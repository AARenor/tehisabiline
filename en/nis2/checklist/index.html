NIS2 compliance checklist: the ten measures, with evidence

Article 21(2) names ten measures as the minimum for every entity in scope. This page turns them into a checklist you can actually run: what “done” looks like, the evidence a reviewer asks for, who owns the row and the failure that repeats. It is a self-assessment, not an audit.

Short answer

Article 21(2) of NIS2 lists ten cyber risk management measures in points (a) to (j). For each one this checklist gives the artefact that proves it exists, the sample that proves it works and the owner who signs it. In Estonia the same duties sit in section 7 of the Cybersecurity Act (KüTS), in force since 1 January 2026. Completing a checklist documents where you stand; it does not by itself satisfy NIS2, because registration, incident reporting and board accountability are separate duties.

Legal basis: what the checklist transcribes

  • Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2), Article 21(2)(a)–(j): the ten measures, applied proportionally to exposure, size and risk.
  • Estonia: KüTS section 7 carries the security measures; section 3 lists who is in scope; section 8 sets the 24-hour, 72-hour and one-month reporting steps.
  • Board accountability: Article 20 and KüTS section 61 — at least one board member approves, oversees and answers for the measures, and RIA can ask for that person's name and contact details.

The measure text below is a substantive English rendering of the directive's own wording. Where Estonian law is narrower or adds a deadline, the Estonian page for each topic states it.

The NIS2 compliance checklist

Ten rows, one per Article 21(2) point. The evidence column is what a reviewer, an auditor or RIA asks to see; the owner column is who signs it off. Any row where you can only show the policy, not the proof, is a gap. Tick a row only when the evidence exists — the progress bar is a self-assessment stored in your own browser, never sent anywhere.

The list is deliberately terse. Proportionality matters: Article 21(1) ties the measures to exposure, size and likelihood of harm, so a 60-person logistics operator and a hospital implement the same ten rows at different depth — but both must be able to show the evidence column.

Board duties are not on the checklist

Article 20 and KüTS section 61 put four duties on the management body: approve the risk management measures, oversee their implementation, be accountable for them and follow training. In practice the reviewer asks two questions — who on the board signed the measures, and when the board last saw the risk picture. RIA may request the designated member's name and contact details, so this is not a formality you can leave to the IT team.

How to run the checklist without wasting a quarter

  1. Score evidence, not intent. Mark a row as done only when the evidence column can be produced from a system, not from memory.
  2. Start with the three rows that pay back fastest. Backup restores (c), MFA (j) and access reviews (i) are cheap, verifiable and reduce real incident cost; they also produce evidence the other rows build on.
  3. Give every row one owner and one date. A row with two owners has none. Put the date in the risk register so the board sees movement.
  4. Test one measure per cycle. Pick a measure, break it on purpose (restore a backup, cut a network path, replay an incident) and attach the result to the row.
  5. Re-score quarterly. Treat the score as a trend, not a certificate: an unchanged score across two quarters usually means nobody is sampling.

Failures that repeat

  • Policy without practice. The document exists, the restore has never been done.
  • MFA on the wrong surface. Rolled out for email, missing on VPN, admin consoles and the service provider's portal.
  • Supplier register without criticality. Every supplier is listed, nothing is marked critical, so no due diligence happens.
  • Training counted by attendance. Completion rates are reported, phishing results are not.
  • No one owns (f). The effectiveness row is the one that turns a self-assessment into evidence, and the one most often left blank.
  • Checklist treated as the finish line. The remaining duties — registration, reporting, board approval — sit outside the ten rows.

Reading the result

The score is a self-assessment and carries no legal weight. Treat it as a conversation tool for the board and as a work sequence:

  • 0–3 rows evidenced: start with (c), (j) and (i). They are fast, observable and they generate artefacts the other rows reuse.
  • 4–7 rows evidenced: the base exists; make (f) real with one independent test and close the reporting process for (b).
  • 8–10 rows evidenced: check freshness instead of adding rows — evidence older than the review cycle is an audit finding waiting to happen.

When the checklist is complete and you need proof that measures operate, the next step is a NIS2 audit: sampled evidence, a findings list and a remediation plan. The Estonian-language version of this checklist, with the same ten measures, is at NIS2 kontrollnimekiri.

Frequently asked questions

What is a NIS2 compliance checklist?

A working list of the ten cyber risk management measures that Article 21(2) of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 names as a minimum. A useful checklist states, for each measure, what “done” looks like, which evidence proves it and who owns it. Ticking a box without evidence only documents a gap.

How many measures does NIS2 require?

Article 21(2) lists ten measures in points (a) to (j): risk analysis and security policy, incident handling, business continuity and backup, supply chain security, security in acquisition and development, effectiveness assessment, cyber hygiene and training, cryptography, access control and asset management, and multi-factor authentication with secure communications. They are a minimum, not a complete control catalogue.

Does the checklist replace a NIS2 audit?

No. A checklist records which measures exist on paper. An audit tests whether they operate: it samples tickets, configuration, backup restores and access reviews. The Estonian regulator RIA may also carry out its own security audits of essential entities.

Is a NIS2 compliance checklist the same as NIS2 requirements?

The requirements are the legal text: Article 21(2) of the directive and, in Estonia, section 7 of the Cybersecurity Act (KüTS). The checklist is a working transcription of those requirements into evidence the entity can collect and show.

Who owns the checklist in the organisation?

The board member designated under Article 20, or the security lead acting under that mandate. Each row still needs a named operational owner: the risk register by the CISO, contracts and supply chain by procurement and legal, backup restores by IT operations.

Does a completed NIS2 checklist prove compliance?

No. Completing the ten measures is one part of NIS2. Registration with RIA, incident reporting within 24 and 72 hours, and board accountability are separate duties, and the measures must be shown to work rather than to exist.
