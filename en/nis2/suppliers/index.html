NIS2 supplier requirements: questionnaire and clauses

NIS2 puts the supply chain duty on you, not on the supplier: you have to know which direct suppliers carry which function, assess their security, record the result and put the expectation into the contract. This page gives the fourteen questions that do most of the work, the clauses that carry them, and a CSV you can send today.

Short answer

Article 21(2)(d) of NIS2 requires supply chain security as part of your risk management, covering the security aspects of the relationship with each direct supplier or service provider. Article 21(3) says what the assessment weighs: the vulnerabilities specific to that supplier, the overall quality of its products and practices, and the results of coordinated risk assessments. There is no supplier certificate and no fixed questionnaire, so the evidence you can produce is the assessment itself — a record, a risk rating and a contract clause with a date on it. In Estonia the same duty sits in section 7 of the Cybersecurity Act (KüTS), in force since 1 January 2026.

What the law requires, and what it leaves to you

Read Article 21(2)(d) with Article 21(3) and the duty becomes concrete in three parts:

  • Know your direct suppliers. Which arrangement supports which function, whether the function is critical or important, and where the service is delivered from. Downstream suppliers of your supplier are covered through the contract, not by you assessing them one by one.
  • Assess, proportionally. The depth follows the exposure: a payment provider that holds customer data deserves more than an office-cleaning contract. Assessment is a record, not a feeling.
  • Contract the expectation. NIS2 does not list mandatory clauses — unlike DORA Article 30 for financial entities' ICT contracts. The clause set follows from the risk you identified, which is why copying another company's contract rarely produces usable evidence.

Two mistakes account for most failed supply chain evidence. The first is a supplier register with a hundred rows and no criticality flags, so nothing was ever assessed. The second is an assessment performed once, before signing, and never revisited when the supplier changed platform, owner or subcontractors.

Contract clauses that carry the evidence

Each clause below exists to make one answer enforceable later. Without it the assessment is a snapshot with no remedy:

  1. Incident notification with a stated timeline and a named route. Your own duty is 24 hours to the early warning and 72 hours to the incident notification after you become aware, so a supplier that notifies in five working days has already cost you the deadline.
  2. Subcontractor disclosure and flow-down: the same security and notification duties pass to subcontractors that underpin the service.
  3. Security requirements by reference to a named standard or control set, with the version, so "industry best practice" is not the standard.
  4. Access and logging obligations, including how logs can be obtained during an investigation.
  5. Audit and evidence rights, proportionate to the criticality — a report may be enough for a low-risk supplier, a site visit may be needed for a critical one.
  6. Data location, return and deletion on exit, with a written confirmation.
  7. Change notification for ownership, location, platform and subcontractors, which is what triggers reassessment.

When you are the supplier receiving the questionnaire

If your customers are in scope, the same duty reaches you as a questionnaire, and the fastest way through it is evidence you already hold: a defined service scope, an access review with dates, one restore test with a log, a patch report, a supplier list with criticality, and a named security contact. Two practical notes:

  • Answer with scope, not with adjectives. "ISO 27001 certified for the Helsinki data centre, certificate valid to 2028, scope excludes the analytics platform" is usable. "We take security seriously" is not.
  • An unanswered question is a red flag for the customer. If something genuinely does not apply, say so and explain why; a documented "not applicable" is cheaper than silence.

For the underlying measures, the NIS2 compliance checklist maps each of the ten duties to the evidence a reviewer asks for. If the questionnaire lands because of a financial-sector customer, the register of information duty sits alongside it — see the DORA register of information.

Running the assessment

  1. Build the register from contracts, not from a spreadsheet of vendor names: one row per contractual arrangement, with the function it supports and a criticality flag.
  2. Send the questionnaire to every supplier supporting a critical or important function, and a short form to the rest.
  3. Rate and record. Low, medium or high, with the reason and the date. The rating is the assessment's output, not a formality.
  4. Close the gap or accept it in writing. A residual risk accepted by a named owner is defensible evidence; an unaddressed high rating is not.
  5. Re-assess on events and annually. Ownership change, new subcontractor, new data category, platform migration — each one invalidates the previous answer.

Where the supplier's answer needs verification rather than reading, a scoped cybersecurity audit or a penetration test of the service interface is the technical evidence; the contract and the register are the management evidence. Neither substitutes for the other.

Frequently asked questions

Do suppliers have to be NIS2 compliant?

There is no supplier certificate and no separate duty that falls on a supplier merely because its customer is in scope. The obligation sits with the entity in scope: Article 21(2)(d) requires it to manage supply chain security, which in practice means assessing direct suppliers, recording the result and putting the expectation into the contract.

What are the NIS2 requirements for suppliers?

Article 21(2)(d) makes supply chain security part of the entity's risk management, and Article 21(3) names what the assessment should weigh: the vulnerabilities specific to each direct supplier, the quality of its products, its cybersecurity practices and the results of coordinated risk assessments. NIS2 does not prescribe a fixed questionnaire or a list of mandatory clauses.

Can we rely on a supplier's ISO 27001 certificate?

Only for the scope the certificate names. An ISO 27001 certificate covers a defined set of services and locations, and it is issued on a management system, not on your specific service. Read the scope statement, check the certification body, note the expiry date, and still ask the questions that concern your data.

What contract clauses should a NIS2 supplier contract contain?

NIS2 does not set a mandatory clause list, so the contractual content follows from the risk assessment: incident notification with a stated timeline, subcontractor disclosure and flow-down of the same duties, access and logging obligations, security requirements tied to a named standard, audit or evidence rights, data location and deletion on exit, and a change-notification duty.

How often should supplier assessments be repeated?

NIS2 sets no fixed cycle. The defensible approach is event-driven plus annual: re-assess a supplier when the service, the data, the subcontractor chain or the ownership changes, and otherwise at least once a year. A supplier with a current certificate still needs the event-driven reassessment.

What if the supplier refuses to answer the questionnaire?

Record the refusal and treat it as risk, not as an obstacle to work around. The evidence of an unanswered question is still evidence: it shows the entity assessed the supplier and documented the gap. For a critical function, an unanswered questionnaire is a reason to look for an alternative, or to reduce the data and access the supplier holds.
