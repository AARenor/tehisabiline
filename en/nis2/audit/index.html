NIS2 audit in Estonia: requirements, evidence and report

NIS2 does not create one mandatory audit certificate. It creates duties that must be evidenced. This guide maps an audit scope to the ten Article 21(2) measures and the Article 20 management duties, and shows which evidence an auditor asks for.

Short answer

NIS2 does not prescribe one audit certificate or one audit format. The obligated entity must be able to evidence the ten measures in Article 21(2)(a) to (j) and management accountability under Article 20. An audit is a structured check of that evidence; a checklist is only a starting point. This page describes an audit method. It is not a certificate, a legal opinion or a formal compliance audit. For the Estonian scope and deadlines, see NIS2 in Estonia.

Each row names what the auditor checks and the evidence to request. Logs are evidence for several rows, not a separate Article 21 measure. The wording is a plain-English summary of Article 21(2) and Article 20.

Is an audit mandatory?

NIS2 does not mandate one certificate or audit form. Estonian law does give RIA a regular audit power over essential entities. KüTS § 16(11)(21), in the version valid from 16 October 2026, reads in Estonian:

„üliolulise üksuse suhtes regulaarseid turvaauditeid üks kord kolme aasta jooksul, välja arvatud juhul, kui ülioluline üksus on kohustatud regulaarselt auditeerima käesoleva seaduse § 7 lõike 5 alusel kehtestatud küberturvalisuse nõuete täitmise raames“

In plain English: RIA may carry out regular security audits of an essential entity once every three years, unless the entity must audit regularly under the cybersecurity requirements set under KüTS § 7(5). Checked 28 October 2026. This is a supervisory power, not a Tehisabiline service, and our cyber audit is not a formal NIS2 compliance audit.

Who can perform a NIS2 audit

NIS2 creates no accredited auditor title, so "NIS2 auditor" is a market label rather than a legal qualification. Three things are called an audit, and they are not interchangeable:

For an external engagement, five questions separate a review from a document exercise: is there a written scope with explicit exclusions; which controls will be tested rather than read; who performs the sampling and what evidence they have handled; how independence is protected when the same provider also implements fixes; and what the deliverable contains. A report that never names an owner, a date or a sample cannot support Article 20 oversight, because the board has nothing to minute.

NIS2 audit checklist: how to prepare

Preparation decides the cost of an audit. Six steps, in order:

  1. Fix the scope in writing. Entities, services, systems, critical suppliers and the reason for each exclusion. An undefined scope is what makes an audit expensive.
  2. Map evidence to the ten measures. Use the NIS2 compliance checklist: one row per Article 21(2) point, with the artefact, the sample and the owner.
  3. Prefer operating evidence over documents. A restore test log, an access review with names and decisions, a closed incident with a timeline. A screenshot without context proves nothing.
  4. Put the board trail together. The minute where the measures were approved, the designated member under KüTS § 61, and the last time the board saw the risk picture.
  5. Check the reporting path end to end. Who detects, who decides it is significant, who submits the 24-hour early warning and the 72-hour notification to RIA, and who writes the final report one month later.
  6. Agree sampling in advance. Sample size, systems, suppliers and the retest date. Undisclosed sampling turns findings into a negotiation.

Requirements an auditor tests most often, and where they break: registration with RIA (three months from becoming obligated, 14 days for a change), the ten measures, the board mandate, and the 24-hour, 72-hour and one-month reporting chain. Fines for failing the measures or reporting reach €10 million or 2% of global turnover for an essential entity and €7 million or 1.4% for an important one — ceilings, not automatic amounts.

Evidence that matters

A policy with no operating evidence is a document, not a control. For each row above, the auditor should see who owns the control, when it was last done, the result and where the record is. A screenshot without context is rarely enough. Logs help prove that a control operates; they are not a separate NIS2 measure.

How an audit runs

  1. Scope: entities, services, systems and critical suppliers.
  2. Evidence request: a short list mapped to the ten duties.
  3. Interviews and sampling: test whether the control operates.
  4. Findings: impact, evidence, fix and owner.
  5. Report: priorities and a retest option.

Start with the registration and incident process if those are not yet documented.

Common gaps

  • Risk register exists, but no treatment dates or owners.
  • MFA is deployed, but coverage is not measured.
  • Backups run, but restore is not tested.
  • Suppliers are listed, but not assessed.
  • Board approval is missing or undated.
  • Incident reporting exists only on paper.

NIS2 audit as an engagement: what the work contains

When the question is "who runs the audit and what does it cost" rather than "what does the law require", the engagement has three parts: a written scope, an examination of process and evidence, and a report with a remediation plan. Tehisabiline delivers this as part of a cybersecurity audit, not as a certification body.

  • Scope document: entities, services, systems, suppliers and the justification for exclusions, agreed in writing before work starts.
  • Sample and tests: risk register, access, a backup restore test, the incident path and supplier evidence.
  • Findings with evidence: each gap carries the evidence seen, the impact and a risk level; unverified scanner noise does not go into the report.
  • Remediation plan: actions prioritised as immediate, 30 days and 90 days, each with an owner and a due date.
  • Management response: the signed decision and accountable person, so the evidence file survives supervisory review.

What drives the price of a NIS2 audit?

There is no public standard price, because scope sets the volume. Cost depends on the number of services and systems, how ready the evidence already is (policy and risk register in place or not), the depth of the supply chain, and whether the outcome must also support an E-ITS or ISO 27001 audit. Scope and price are confirmed in writing before work begins.

What we do not do

We do not issue NIS2 certificates, legal opinions or act for you before the RIA. Where an accredited certification audit under ISO 27001 or E-ITS is required, that has to come from a certification body.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a NIS2 audit cost?

There is no public standard price, because scope sets the volume: the number of services and systems, how ready the evidence already is, the depth of the supply chain, and whether the outcome must also support an E-ITS or ISO 27001 audit. Scope and price are confirmed in writing before work begins.

Does NIS2 require a mandatory audit certificate?

No. NIS2 does not create one mandatory audit certificate. Under Estonian law, RIA may carry out regular security audits of an essential entity once every three years (KüTS, section 16, subsection 11, point 21), unless the entity must audit regularly under cybersecurity requirements set under KüTS, section 7, subsection 5. Supervisors and customers can ask you to evidence that the ten duties operate; an internal or independent audit can produce that evidence.

What does a NIS2 audit cover?

The ten Article 21(2) measures (a) to (j): risk analysis and security policy; incident handling; business continuity, backup and crisis management; supply-chain security; secure acquisition, development and maintenance, including vulnerability handling; assessing the effectiveness of measures; basic cyber hygiene and training; cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption; HR security, access control and asset management; and MFA or continuous authentication, secured communications and emergency communications. Management duties under Article 20 are assessed separately.

Is a checklist enough?

No. A checklist shows intent. An audit checks evidence that the control operates, with an owner, date and result.

How long does it take?

It depends on scope and evidence quality. A focused review can take days; a multi-entity group with weak evidence takes longer. Agree the scope in writing first.

Who can perform a NIS2 audit?

NIS2 creates no accredited auditor qualification, so anyone competent in the ten Article 21(2) measures and in evidence handling can carry out a review. What matters is a written scope, tested rather than read controls, and independence from the fixes being recommended. RIA's own security audit of an essential entity is a supervisory power under KüTS § 16(11)(21), not something the entity commissions.

Is an internal NIS2 audit enough?

An internal review satisfies the Article 21(2)(f) duty to assess the effectiveness of the measures, and it is cheaper. It is weaker as evidence for a board, a customer or a supervisor, because the same team both operates and judges the controls. Many entities alternate: internal review each quarter, one external review per year.

What should a NIS2 audit checklist contain?

One row per Article 21(2) measure with the evidence the auditor will request, the sample that proves the control operates, the owner and the date. Preparation steps — written scope, board trail, reporting chain, agreed sampling — belong in a separate list. The ten-measure version is at NIS2 compliance checklist.
