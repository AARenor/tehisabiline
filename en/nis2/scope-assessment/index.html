NIS2 scope assessment: are you in scope?

Two questions decide almost every case: is your activity a listed type, and are you big enough? Two more overrule the size answer. This page works the test in four steps, runs it as an eight-question tool in your browser, and gives you a worksheet the board can sign.

Short answer

NIS2 applies when a listed type of activity meets a size test, or when a special case switches the size test off. Two articles do the work. Article 2 says who is covered: entities of a type referred to in Annex I or Annex II that qualify as medium-sized enterprises, or exceed the medium-sized ceilings, and which provide services in the Union. Article 3 splits the covered entities into essential and important. In Estonia the same test sits in the Cybersecurity Act (KüTS), in force since 1 January 2026, and RIA is the authority you notify. The eight-question tool on this page runs the test locally, and the scope assessment worksheet turns the answer into a signed page.

Article 2 decides whether you have duties at all; Article 3 decides how closely you will be supervised and how large the maximum fine is.

The legal test in four steps

Run the steps in order. The first two can only narrow the answer, the fourth can only widen it, and the third decides which figures you are allowed to use.

  1. Type of activity. Compare what you actually do with the sector and subsector lists in Annex I and Annex II. The annexes describe entities by reference to other Union law or by a named role, so the test is the definition, not the trading name or a broad statistical code. A software vendor or a consultancy is not on either list; a managed service provider, a data centre operator, a hospital, a water utility or a research organisation is.
  2. Size. Article 2(1) reaches entities that qualify as medium-sized enterprises, or that exceed the ceilings for medium-sized enterprises, under Article 2 of the Annex to Recommendation 2003/361/EC. The same article switches off Article 3(4) of that Annex, so the rule that a company controlled by public bodies cannot be an SME does not apply here: public ownership does not remove you from the count. The figures are the latest approved accounting period, counted in annual work units, and the ceilings have to be exceeded over two consecutive accounting periods before your class changes.
  3. Group treatment. The SME Annex is not a standalone-accounts test. Article 6 of its Annex adds the data of partner enterprises in proportion to the capital or voting interest and 100 per cent of the data of linked enterprises, unless those data are already consolidated. Estonia then adds a qualification: KüTS § 3(7) disregards partner and linked-enterprise data where the entity is independent of them in the systems used to provide its services. Both directions matter — a small subsidiary inside a large group is not automatically covered, and a group that consolidates its figures is not automatically outside.
  4. Special cases. Article 2(2) to (4) list situations that apply regardless of size, and Article 2(5) lists two categories Member States may add. They override a “too small” conclusion, so check them before you write anything down. Article 2(7) is the opposite lever: activities in national security, public security, defence or law enforcement are outside the directive.

Once you are covered, Article 3 classifies you. Essential entities are the Annex I types that exceed the medium-sized ceilings, qualified trust service providers, top-level domain name registries and DNS service providers, public electronic communications providers that qualify as medium-sized enterprises, central government public administration entities, entities identified as critical under Directive (EU) 2022/2557, and any entity a Member State designates as essential under Article 2(2)(b) to (e). Everything else of a listed type is an important entity. The label changes supervision and the ceiling on fines, not the ten security measures.

Size thresholds: essential, important and out of scope

The figures are the SME ceilings from Recommendation 2003/361/EC, applied at two levels: below the small band the directive does not reach you unless a special case applies, and above the medium-sized ceilings an Annex I entity becomes essential.

Estonian summaries often phrase the entry test as “50 employees and €10 million” as if both were needed at once. The Recommendation states the small-enterprise test as both conditions together, so exceeding either ceiling leaves the small band. If the two readings differ for you, record which one you applied and confirm it with RIA: it decides whether you register at all. The NIS2 in Estonia overview carries the national thresholds and the dates that follow from them.

Two consequences follow: one company can sit in both annexes through different services, and annex membership alone is not enough, because Article 2(1) applies the size test to both annexes and only Article 2(2) to (4) remove it.

Special cases that override size

Several provisions put a small provider in scope, and one takes a large one out. All are short enough to check in a single meeting.

The Estonian list is broader in places because KüTS also names providers of vital services (elutähtsa teenuse osutaja) designated under the crisis regime, and treats public-law legal persons as important entities without a size test. Treat the national list as the operative one for a company registered in Estonia and the directive as the floor; the overview page sets out both.

Run the assessment

Eight yes or no answers, computed in your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere and no answer is stored. Answer for the entity that would register with RIA, not for the group's headline figures.

1. Is your main activity a type of entity listed in Annex I?

Energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, health, drinking water, waste water, digital infrastructure, ICT service management (managed service providers), public administration, space.

2. Is your main activity a type of entity listed in Annex II?

Postal and courier services, waste management, chemicals, food, manufacturing, digital providers (marketplaces, search engines, social networks), research organisations.

3. Do you employ 50 or more people (annual work units)?

Use the latest approved accounting period. Fewer than 50 is the small-enterprise headcount ceiling in the Annex to Recommendation 2003/361/EC.

4. Is your annual turnover above €10 million, or your balance sheet total above €10 million?

Turnover excluding VAT. Exceeding either figure leaves the small-enterprise band, even when the headcount stays below 50.

5. Would partner and linked enterprises take you over the threshold?

Partner data are added in proportion to the capital or voting interest, linked data in full. In Estonia, KüTS § 3(7) disregards them where you are independent of them in the systems used to provide your services.

6. Do you provide a service that is covered regardless of size?

Public electronic communications network or service, trust service, top-level domain name registry, DNS service provider, domain name registration, or an entity identified as critical under Directive (EU) 2022/2557.

7. Are you a public administration entity?

A central government body, or a regional-level body providing services whose disruption could significantly affect critical societal or economic activities.

8. Do you exceed the medium-sized ceilings?

250 employees or more, or turnover above €50 million, or balance sheet total above €43 million. This decides essential versus important, not whether you are in scope at all.

After you conclude that you are in scope

Four duties start at the same moment. The shortest clock is the one most often forgotten.

  1. Register with RIA. A new service provider must notify RIA within three months of meeting the criteria (KüTS § 41(1)), and any change in the submitted data within two weeks. Notification goes through the business view of eesti.ee under Küberturvalisus → Teenuseosutaja teavitus. Entities already in scope on 1 January 2026 had three months from that date; that window closed in spring 2026, so if you have not notified, notify now and record why it was late. The step-by-step walk-through is on NIS2 registration in Estonia.
  2. Apply the ten measures. Article 21(2) names risk analysis and policy, incident handling, business continuity and backup, supply-chain security, security in acquisition and development, effectiveness assessment, cyber hygiene and training, cryptography, access control and asset management, and multi-factor authentication. Each one needs an owner and an artefact: the compliance checklist is the working version.
  3. Put a name on the board. Article 20 requires the management body to approve, oversee and answer for the measures; under KüTS § 61 a designated board member carries that duty, and RIA may ask who that is.
  4. Prepare the reporting chain. A significant incident triggers an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 hours and a final report within one month of the 72-hour filing. The sequence and the content of each step are on NIS2 incident reporting.

The consequence of getting it wrong is administrative, not theoretical. Maximum fines run to €10 million or 2% of the previous financial year's worldwide annual turnover for essential entities, and €7 million or 1.4% for important entities, whichever is higher — caps, not predictions, and the supervisory measures can bite harder than the number. NIS2 fines and liability sets out both. If you want a certification framework to carry the evidence, ISO 27001 in Estonia maps onto the same ten areas — though a certificate is not a scope decision and does not replace registration.

Common misreadings

  • “Our customers are in scope, so we are.” Suppliers are not brought in by their customers' status. Your own activity type and your own size decide. What does travel down the chain is contract terms and due-diligence demands, because Article 21(2)(d) makes the in-scope entity responsible for supply-chain risk.
  • “We are part of a group, so the group’s turnover decides.” Aggregation exists, but it is governed by Article 6 of the SME Annex, and Estonia qualifies it in KüTS § 3(7). Neither “we are small” nor “we are inside a big group” is an answer on its own.
  • “Under 50 staff means we are out.” The special cases in Article 2(2) to (4) exist precisely for small providers, and a company of 20 people running a DNS service or a qualified trust service is essential.
  • “Being in scope means being essential.” Most entities in scope are important entities. The label changes supervision and the fine ceiling, not the ten measures.
  • “A certificate settles it.” An ISO 27001 certificate or an E-ITS implementation shows how you manage risk; it does not decide whether Article 2 applies, and it does not replace the notification.
  • “We are a software vendor, so we are covered as a digital provider.” Annex II covers online marketplaces, search engines and social networks — not software licensing. A vendor becomes relevant as a managed service provider only when it operates services for clients.

Frequently asked questions

What is a NIS2 scope assessment?

A written test of whether Directive (EU) 2022/2555 applies to you, and whether you would be an essential or an important entity. It records the sector or type of entity from Annex I or Annex II, the staff and financial figures, the group treatment, the conclusion and the person who signed it. One page is enough; what matters is that the reasoning is written down before an incident, not after one.

Which entities are in scope of NIS2?

Entities of a type listed in Annex I or Annex II that qualify as medium-sized enterprises or exceed the medium-sized ceilings, plus entities covered regardless of size under Article 2(2), critical entities identified under Directive (EU) 2022/2557 and providers of domain name registration services. Public or private ownership does not matter; the activity and the size do.

What are the NIS2 size thresholds?

The directive uses the SME definition in the Annex to Recommendation 2003/361/EC. A small enterprise employs fewer than 50 persons and has an annual turnover and/or a balance sheet total not exceeding €10 million; a medium-sized enterprise employs fewer than 250 persons with turnover not exceeding €50 million and/or a balance sheet total not exceeding €43 million. Leaving the small band brings you into scope; exceeding the medium-sized ceilings makes an Annex I entity essential.

Does the NIS2 size test include the whole group?

Usually it must be considered. The SME Annex aggregates the data of partner enterprises in proportion to the capital or voting interest and 100 per cent of the data of linked enterprises. In Estonia, KüTS section 3(7) disregards partner and linked-enterprise data where the entity is independent of them in the systems used to provide its services, so a small subsidiary of a large group is neither automatically out of scope nor automatically in it.

Are suppliers of NIS2 entities in scope?

Not automatically. A supplier is in scope only if its own activity is a type listed in Annex I or Annex II and its own size reaches the threshold, or if a provision such as Article 2(2) applies to it. Article 21(2)(d) nevertheless pushes security requirements down the supply chain, because the entity in scope must manage the risk created by its direct suppliers and service providers.

Can a small company be in scope of NIS2?

Yes. Article 2(2) covers several categories regardless of size: providers of public electronic communications networks or publicly available electronic communications services, trust service providers, top-level domain name registries and DNS service providers, an entity that is the sole provider of a service essential for critical societal or economic activities, and an entity whose disruption could significantly affect public safety, public security or public health. Critical entities under Directive (EU) 2022/2557 and providers of domain name registration services are also covered regardless of size.

What happens after you conclude that you are in scope?

Notify RIA, in Estonia, within three months of becoming a service provider in scope, then apply the ten measures of Article 21(2), designate the accountable board member under Article 20 and prepare the 24-hour and 72-hour incident reports. Registration is a notification, not a certificate: RIA supervises, and the maximum fine for an essential entity is €10 million or 2% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.
