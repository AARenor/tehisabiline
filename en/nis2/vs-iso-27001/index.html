NIS2 vs ISO 27001: mapping Article 21(2) to Annex A

Both documents are about information security management. Only one of them is law. This page maps the ten measures of Article 21(2) onto the 93 controls of ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A, marks what an existing ISMS already evidences, and names the three duties a certificate cannot cover.

Short answer

No. An ISO/IEC 27001 certificate cannot prove NIS2 compliance. Certification attests that an information security management system conforms to a voluntary international standard, audited by an accredited certification body against a scope the organisation defines. NIS2 is a directive — in Estonia transposed by the Cybersecurity Act (KüTS) and supervised by RIA — and it carries duties that no management system contains: registration with the competent authority, an early warning within 24 hours and an incident notification within 72 hours of a significant incident, and management-body approval, training and liability under Article 20. The ISMS does carry the substance of most Article 21(2) measures, which is why the mapping below is worth doing. It cannot carry the legal acts.

Why the two are compared at all

They get compared because both appear in the same procurement questionnaire, and because the comparison misleads in one direction: it makes a certificate look like a licence.

ISO/IEC 27001 is a management system standard. Its requirements are in clauses 4 to 10 — context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, improvement — plus 93 controls in Annex A, in four themes: organisational 5.1–5.37 (37 controls), people 6.1–6.8 (8), physical 7.1–7.14 (14) and technological 8.1–8.34 (34). Which controls apply, and how, is decided by the organisation through risk assessment (clause 6.1.2) and risk treatment (6.1.3), then recorded in the statement of applicability. Nothing in the standard is law anywhere.

NIS2, Directive (EU) 2022/2555, is law. It obliges Member States to impose duties on essential and important entities: the cybersecurity risk-management measures of Article 21, the reporting obligations of Article 23, the governance duties of Article 20 and registration through a national mechanism. In Estonia those duties sit in KüTS and the supervisor is RIA, which sets the supervisory practice and applies the sanctions in national law.

The mapping, Article 21(2) point by point

Ten rows, one per point of Article 21(2). Full means a certification audit already samples the evidence for that point, so an existing ISMS mostly answers it. Partly means the standard requires the mechanism but not the legal effect; the last column names what is still missing. Three notes on reading the table. Control numbers are those of ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A; the implementation guidance for each is in ISO/IEC 27002:2022, which carries the same 93 controls, so the titles are worth checking there before you cite one in a statement of applicability. "Full" is a statement about evidence, not about legal sufficiency — a supervisor may still ask for a NIS2-specific document. And the mapping is per point, not per control: one control such as 8.5 satisfies part of two points, which is why the CSV lists it twice.

Where an Estonian public body also follows E-ITS, the national framework decides the detail and the ISO mapping becomes a translation layer rather than the source; see E-ITS in Estonia. The supplier row (d) is where certificates most often over-promise, so it has its own guide: NIS2 supplier requirements.

What an ISO 27001 ISMS gives you for free

If the ISMS already exists, most of Article 21(2) is not new work. Five artefacts are directly reusable:

  • A risk method you are allowed to reuse. Clause 6.1.2 requires risks to be identified, analysed and evaluated against criteria you set, and clause 8.2 requires the assessment to be repeated. Point (a) asks for policies on risk analysis; the method and the register already exist.
  • The statement of applicability as your NIS2 mapping document. Clause 6.1.3 requires comparing the controls you selected with Annex A and justifying exclusions. That is exactly the artefact a reviewer wants when you claim a measure is not applicable — and the artefact most entities write twice when they build NIS2 evidence from nothing.
  • An audit cycle the board can read. Internal audit (clause 9.2), management review (clause 9.3) and corrective action (clause 10.2) give point (f) its content: effectiveness is tested, minuted and tracked rather than asserted.
  • Supplier discipline. 5.19 to 5.22 require supplier relationships to be governed: security measures agreed, clauses documented and supplier services monitored. Point (d) builds on that framework rather than inventing one.
  • The people, access and incident blocks. 6.1 to 6.8, 5.9, 5.15 to 5.18 and 8.2 to 8.3 cover points (b), (g) and (i) in substance: joiners, movers, leavers, access reviews, inventories, event reporting, and the logging and monitoring in 8.15 and 8.16 that start the clock on any reporting duty.

Add document control (clause 7.5) and the set becomes traceable: every artefact above carries an identifier, a version and a date, which is what separates evidence from a folder of PDFs.

The three gaps an ISMS does not close

These are not weaknesses in ISO/IEC 27001; they are outside its subject. No amount of Annex A work produces them, and each has its own owner and its own clock.

1. Reporting within 24 hours and 72 hours

Article 23 requires an early warning without undue delay and in any event within 24 hours of becoming aware of a significant incident, an incident notification within 72 hours, and a final report within one month of the notification. The addressee is the CSIRT or, where applicable, the competent authority; in Estonia the incident route runs through CERT-EE. Annex A contains no external notification duty, so an ISMS incident process usually stops at internal escalation. What closes it: a runbook that names the addressee, the content of each step and the person who files it, plus log retention long enough to reconstruct when awareness began. The steps are set out in NIS2 incident reporting.

2. Registration with RIA

Registration is an administrative act: name and registry code, address, current contact details, IP ranges and the services in scope, notified to RIA in the state portal eesti.ee. In Estonia the notification is due within three months of becoming a service provider within KüTS section 3, and changes to the registered data within two weeks. ISO/IEC 27001 has nothing resembling it, because a management system does not care whether anyone knows you exist. An entity can hold a valid certificate and never have registered. What closes it: the notification on file with RIA, a named owner and a calendar entry for changes. See registration with RIA.

3. Board training and accountability

Article 20 requires the management body to approve the measures taken under Article 21, oversee their implementation and be liable for infringements, and requires its members to follow training. ISO/IEC 27001 requires top-management commitment (clause 5.1) and an approved information security policy (clause 5.2) — adjacent, but not the same act: the certificate records no designated member, no board training record and no liability. Point (g) training is aimed at staff, and control 6.3 follows suit. What closes it: a board minute approving the measures, a training record with dates and content, and the name of the member RIA can contact.

Order of work when you have neither

The sequence below assumes no ISMS and no registration. It front-loads the duties with the shortest fuse and the lowest dependency count.

  1. Fix the boundary once. Decide which services put you in scope and write that down as the ISMS scope (clause 4.3). Two boundaries mean two sets of evidence for the same control.
  2. Register with RIA. No dependency on the ISMS, no technical work, and the cheapest unmet duty on the list.
  3. Write the incident process next. The 24-hour and 72-hour clocks are the shortest fuse. Everything else tolerates a quarter; reporting does not.
  4. Assess risk over the in-scope services (6.1.2), then write the treatment plan and the statement of applicability (6.1.3). This document doubles as your Article 21(2) mapping; the CSV on this page is its checklist.
  5. Close the "partly" rows in the order (b), (d), (e), (j). Those are the points where a certificate buys the least coverage per euro, so they need work of their own.
  6. Run one internal audit and one management review (9.2, 9.3) before inviting a certification body. Both are cheaper and shorter once the evidence exists, and the audit is the step a supervisor notices first.

Certificate or compliance — which is the goal?

They are different products. Compliance is a legal state: registration, measures proportionate to exposure and size, reporting readiness, and a management body that answers for all of it. RIA assesses it, and it can be held with no certificate at all. Certification is a market signal and an audit cadence: an accredited body confirms conformity with ISO/IEC 27001 inside a defined scope, and that can be held while registration or reporting has been neglected.

For most entities in scope the order is compliance first, certificate second, because the certificate is optional and the duties are not. Where a tender, a customer or a group policy demands ISO/IEC 27001, run both at once with one risk register, one supplier register and one internal audit programme. The certification path is described in ISO 27001 in Estonia, the evidence per measure in the NIS2 compliance checklist, and what a reviewer samples in the NIS2 audit. The wider legal picture, including DORA, the CRA and the AI Act, is in cybersecurity regulations in Estonia.

Frequently asked questions

Does ISO 27001 certification prove NIS2 compliance?

No. ISO/IEC 27001 attests that an information security management system conforms to a voluntary standard, within a scope the organisation defines. NIS2 adds duties that sit outside any management system: registration with the competent authority, an early warning within 24 hours and an incident notification within 72 hours, and management-body accountability under Article 20.

Which Annex A controls map to Article 21(2)?

All ten points have an answer in Annex A, but only four map fully: risk analysis and policy to 5.1, 5.2 and the risk clauses 6.1.2 and 6.1.3; effectiveness assessment to 9.1, 9.2, 9.3 and 5.35; training and cyber hygiene to 6.3; and HR security, access control and asset management to 5.9, 5.15 to 5.18 and 8.1 to 8.3. The other six map partly, and the downloadable CSV lists the controls and the gap for every point.

What does NIS2 require that ISO 27001 does not?

Registration through the national mechanism, an early warning within 24 hours and an incident notification within 72 hours of becoming aware of a significant incident, a final report within one month, and management-body approval, training and liability for the measures. Article 21(3) also requires the vulnerabilities specific to each direct supplier to be taken into account when deciding which supply chain measures are appropriate.

Can a certification body certify NIS2 compliance?

No. Certification bodies are accredited against standards such as ISO/IEC 27001 and their finding is conformity with that standard. NIS2 compliance is determined by the competent authority, in Estonia RIA, through supervision. A certificate can be evidence in that assessment; it cannot be the assessment.

Do we need ISO 27001 if we are in NIS2 scope?

Not legally. ISO/IEC 27001 is voluntary. It is useful because it supplies a documented risk method, an internal audit cycle and a control set that already covers most of Article 21(2), which is cheaper than building the same evidence twice. Whether to certify is a commercial question set by customers, tenders and group policy.

What order should a company with neither start in?

Fix the scope, register with RIA, then write the incident process with the 24-hour and 72-hour steps, because that clock is the shortest. Build the risk register over the in-scope services and let the statement of applicability serve as the Article 21(2) mapping. Invite a certification body last: the audit is shorter once the evidence exists.

Should the ISMS scope match the NIS2 scope?

It should. Clause 4.3 lets the organisation define the ISMS scope, and a certificate commonly covers a named set of locations and services. NIS2 follows the service, so a scope that stops short of an in-scope service creates evidencing work the certificate cannot answer.
