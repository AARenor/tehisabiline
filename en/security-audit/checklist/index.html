Short answer

A cybersecurity audit tests whether the controls an organisation claims to have exist, operate and leave evidence. A penetration test tests whether a specific weakness can be exploited right now. The audit reads artefacts — board minutes, access reviews, restore logs, ticket trails, contract schedules — and samples them; the test runs tools against an agreed target and writes up the exploit. Neither implies the other. An audit can find no exceptions while an exploit still works, and a test can find nothing while the last successful backup restore happened years ago.

Everything below assumes the first kind of work. The checklist is a sampling plan: each row names the evidence that decides it, so a review takes hours rather than weeks. The report template fixes the anatomy of a finding, because a finding that cannot be retested cannot be closed.

Scope choices: audit, technical assessment, pentest, gap analysis

A scope that says "the whole company" cannot be audited, and a report written against it cannot be defended. Write down three things instead: the systems and sites included by name, the evidence period covered, and the boundaries — group services, hosting providers, the parts of the estate nobody can log. The remaining uncertainty belongs in the limitations section, not in a footnote nobody reads. If the legal driver is NIS2, the same evidence pack also serves the NIS2 audit route; if it is certification, start from the ISO 27001 audit checklist and use this page for the operational rows.

The checklist by domain

Ten domains, 74 rows. Each row carries the control area, the question put to the evidence owner, the artefact requested, the failure we see most often behind that row, an empty result column and a default severity for the case where the row fails. The result column is yours: pass, partial, fail or not applicable — with the evidence reference written next to it.

How to sample evidence without reading every ticket

An audit is a sample, and the sample is defensible only if it came from a list. Six rules cover most of the work.

  1. Get the population first. Ask for the complete list — every account with administrator rights, every endpoint, every supplier, every backup job — and sample from that list. Sampling from the items the client remembers produces a finding about memory, not about the control.
  2. Name the method. NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5 defines three assessment methods — examine, interview and test — and two attributes that set the effort: depth and coverage. A row assessed by interview alone is weaker evidence than the same row tested on a live path, and the report should say which one was used.
  3. Take all of the high-risk items. Privileged accounts, internet-facing admin interfaces, backup jobs for critical systems and critical suppliers are small populations. Cover every one of them, then take a random sample of the rest. In our audits, a sample of 10 to 25 items per control area is normally enough to separate a designed control from an operating one.
  4. Spread the sample over the period, not over last week. Evidence prepared for a visit is evidence of preparation. Pull items across the whole audit period, including the month when the key person was on leave.
  5. Prefer machine evidence. A configuration export, a log query or a console report carries a timestamp and cannot be edited as easily as a policy PDF. Where an export would move personal data, ask for read-only access instead of a copy.
  6. Apply a stop rule. One exception is a sample exception. Two independent items failing the same way makes it a design finding, and the answer is a recommendation about the process rather than about those two items. Write the rule into the method section so the reader can see it was applied consistently.

Keep an evidence register while you work: request reference, who was asked, when, what arrived, the file name and its hash, and who holds the original. Half of the disputes about an audit report end when the register shows what was requested and what came back.

Writing the report

A finding needs a shape before it needs prose: condition (what is), criteria (what should be, quoted from a named source), cause (why it exists), effect (what happens if nobody acts), evidence (file reference and date), recommendation (action, target state, completion test), owner and due date. Anything missing turns the row into an opinion — and an opinion cannot be retested, disputed or closed.

Severity

For exploitable weaknesses, use CVSS v4.0 and its qualitative ratings: none 0.0, low 0.1 to 3.9, medium 4.0 to 6.9, high 7.0 to 8.9, critical 9.0 to 10.0. Two caveats matter in an audit. First, CVSS describes the intrinsic severity of a vulnerability, not the business impact of that vulnerability in your estate, so the score cannot be the whole rating. Second, the specification states that publishing those qualitative ratings is optional — which means the report has to define its own scale anyway for the rows that have no vector at all, such as a supplier register without criticality. Define four levels in the method section, apply them to every finding, and keep the definition stable between audits so the trend means something.

Phrasing that survives a retest

A recommendation names an outcome and the test that proves it. It does not name a product, and it does not ask for attention.

Weak phrasingFinding-ready phrasing
Improve backup arrangements and consider testing more often.Restore the finance database from the offsite copy into an isolated environment by 28 November 2026 and attach the restore log; verified by the CISO.
MFA should be reviewed across all systems.Enforce MFA in the cloud tenant for all accounts holding an administrator role by 15 December 2026, evidenced by a policy export showing zero excluded admin accounts.
Access rights are not optimal.Remove permanent administrator rights from the helpdesk group by 30 November 2026 and attach the next quarterly access review showing the change retained.
The supplier register needs updating.Rate every supplier in the register as critical, important or standard, with an owner per row, by 31 January 2027; the completion test is a register export with no blank criticality field.

Two habits keep the rest of the document honest. Findings are ordered by severity and never by the order they were discovered. And every finding states what it does not cover, because a reader who assumes wider coverage will make a decision the evidence does not support.

What the audited team should prepare

  • One coordinator and one owner per domain. The coordinator keeps the request log and decides who answers what. Without it, requests fan out to five people and three of them answer the same question differently.
  • Population lists before anything else. Assets, identities, privileged accounts, suppliers, backup jobs, tickets. The audit starts when the lists arrive, not when the kickoff meeting ends.
  • Read-only access instead of exports. It minimises the personal data that leaves your environment and it is faster than exporting six months of tickets.
  • Do not clean up first. Deleting old accounts or editing logs the week before a review is itself a finding, and a worse one than the original gap. Disclose known gaps with a plan; disclosed and scheduled reads as management, discovered reads as concealment.
  • Send the person who runs the process. Interviews with whoever wrote the policy, and no one else, produce a report about the policy.
  • Have the awkward documents ready. Last year's audit or pentest report with open items, the incident log, the board minutes, the contracts with the three suppliers you would rather not discuss. They will be requested, and producing them in the first hour sets the tone for the rest.

Reading the result: what a good report never says

Some phrases mark a report you can act on. Their absence is the signal:

  • "Fully compliant." An audit reports on a scope against named criteria. Compliance is a legal or certification decision taken by someone else.
  • "No risks found." A sample of 20 items cannot support that sentence, and a report that writes it has stopped describing its own method.
  • "Best practice" as the only criterion. Either a standard, a law or your own policy is cited, or the finding is a preference.
  • "Critical" without a stated effect. Severity without impact is decoration.
  • A percentage without a population. "94% patched" means nothing until it names the denominator and the report date.
  • A recommendation naming a tool. The test is the outcome, not the purchase.

Read the report in this order: scope and limitations, then the findings at critical and high, then the risk summary, then the roadmap last. The first section tells you what the rest is entitled to claim. When a finding looks wrong, ask for the sample and the evidence reference rather than arguing about the conclusion — either the evidence exists and the finding stands, or it does not and the finding comes out. That is the whole dispute.

Before you close the audit, check that every row in the checklist has a result and an evidence reference, that every finding has an owner and a date, and that the retest scope is written down. If the next review is meant to measure progress, the comparison only works when this one recorded where it started.

Frequently asked questions

People ask for this artefact as a cyber security audit checklist, an IT security audit checklist or a security audit report template. It is one piece of work under three names: the controls an audit tests, the evidence that decides each row, and a report format that lets a finding be retested by someone else.

What is a cybersecurity audit checklist?

A list of the controls an audit will test, with the evidence that proves each one operates and the typical failure found behind it. It is a sampling plan, not a compliance certificate: a ticked row means evidence was produced for that row, and nothing more.

How is a cybersecurity audit checklist different from a penetration test report?

The checklist asks whether a control exists and operates across the organisation, and it samples artefacts such as access reviews, restore logs and contract schedules. A penetration test report records whether a specific weakness could be exploited inside an agreed scope. A clean audit does not imply a secure system, and a clean test does not imply a working control.

What should a security audit report template contain?

Nine parts: cover with the independence statement, scope and boundaries, method with the evidence register, findings in a fixed block, risk summary, remediation roadmap with owners and dates, retest rules, an appendix of sampled evidence, and sign-off. The finding block needs condition, criteria, cause, effect, evidence reference, recommendation and owner.

How many items should you sample in a security audit?

Enough to tell a designed control from an operating one, drawn from a complete population list rather than from memory. That usually means every high-risk item, such as privileged accounts, critical suppliers and internet-facing admin interfaces, plus a random sample of the rest, spread across the audit period and not only the last week.

Can this checklist be used for an ISO 27001 internal audit?

Partly. The domains overlap with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and its Annex A controls, but an internal audit is judged against the standard's clauses and your statement of applicability. Use this checklist for the operational evidence and map each row to the clause or control you audit against.

What severity scale should audit findings use?

For exploitable weaknesses, CVSS v4.0 with its qualitative ratings: none 0.0, low 0.1 to 3.9, medium 4.0 to 6.9, high 7.0 to 8.9, critical 9.0 to 10.0. Governance findings have no vector, so define four levels in the report itself and apply them to every finding the same way.

Who should prepare the evidence for a security audit?

The owner of each domain, coordinated by one person who keeps the evidence register. IT should not write the governance evidence, and the board should not collect its own minutes. One named owner per row, one named coordinator for the request log, and read-only access wherever an export would move personal data.
