TLPT and TIBER-EU: threat-led penetration testing under DORA

Article 26 of DORA requires threat-led penetration testing from the financial entities that their competent authority identifies. TIBER-EU is the framework that describes the test and TIBER-EE is Estonia’s national implementation. This page covers the phases in order, who is actually obliged, what the report proves to a supervisor, a board and an auditor, and where the exercise differs from a penetration test or red teaming.

Short answer

TLPT (threat-led penetration testing) is the advanced test Article 26 of DORA requires: an intelligence-led, controlled attack on the live production systems behind a financial entity’s critical or important functions. TIBER-EU is the European framework that describes how such a test is run — developed by the ECB with the national central banks, published in May 2018 and updated in 2024 to align with the DORA technical standards on TLPT. TIBER-EE is Estonia’s national implementation, an advisory guide from Finantsinspektsioon in force since 15 September 2026.

You do not put yourself in scope: Article 26(1) applies to entities the competent authority has identified under Article 26(8) by supervisory decision, at least every three years. The output is not a pass mark but a test summary report, a remediation plan and an attestation that the test followed the framework.

TLPT vs penetration test vs red teaming

The three terms get used as synonyms in procurement documents and produce very different artefacts. What matters legally is who fixes the scope, who knows the test is running, and what the deliverable proves.

Red teaming is the technique; TLPT is the regulated process around it. A red-team exercise without threat intelligence, a validated scope and closure artefacts is legitimate testing, but not TLPT. A penetration test is useful preparation and cannot substitute for it.

The TIBER-EU phases step by step

A TIBER-EU test runs as a preparation phase, a testing phase with two components and a closure phase, each with named artefacts the TLPT authority reviews — which is what makes mutual recognition possible. The Estonian guide adds governance, not a second methodology.

  1. Preparation. Starts with a written notification from the authority to your designated contact point and covers four steps: notification, initiation, scoping and procurement. Artefacts: initiation documents, an initial risk assessment with safeguards that limit impact on production, a scope specification document naming the critical or important functions with the systems and ICT providers behind them, and the procured threat intelligence provider and red team tester.
  2. Testing: threat intelligence. The provider collects target intelligence (attack surface, exposures) and threat intelligence (relevant actors, probable scenarios) and turns it into a targeted threat intelligence report. You agree the scenarios with the test manager at a scenario selection meeting; the report is approved before any attack begins.
  3. Testing: red team testing. The red team writes a test plan against the approved scenarios, then tests actively towards the flags in that plan, imitating a real adversary. Progress goes to the control team and test manager in weekly updates, and every action is logged for the replay.
  4. Closure. The red team report and the blue team report are produced — the second by people who did not know the test was running — then a replay or purple-teaming exercise compares what was and was not detected, followed by the test summary report, the remediation plan, 360-degree feedback and the attestation.

Coordination sits with TCT-EE, the TLPT cyber team inside Finantsinspektsioon’s financial sector IT supervision department, with test managers assigned per test; FI validates key artefacts and issues the attestation. A Generic Threat Landscape may be published as a supporting input that does not replace test-specific intelligence, and information is handled under an agreed classification such as the Traffic Light Protocol.

Who is obliged, and on what reasoning

Article 26(1) applies to financial entities other than those referred to in the first subparagraph of Article 16(1) and other than microenterprises, and only once the competent authority has identified them under Article 26(8). The authority applies the criteria of Article 4(2) and weighs three factors: how far your services affect the financial sector, whether there are financial stability or systemic concerns at national or Union level, and your ICT risk profile or maturity. The frequency is at least every three years, and the authority may shorten or lengthen that interval when the risk profile and circumstances justify it.

Estonia: who supervises what

  • Finantsinspektsioon (FI) acts as the TLPT authority under Article 26(9) for the financial entities in its TLPT remit, and issues the attestation for those tests.
  • The ECB is the TLPT authority for mandatory TLPTs of significant institutions under its direct supervision, applies its TIBER-EU SSM guide to them, and always issues the attestation.
  • Other Estonian financial entities may run a TIBER-EE test voluntarily, subject to prior agreement with FI. Other sectors may apply TIBER-EU on their own initiative, outside the Estonian guide and FI’s TLPT governance.

The significant-institution difference

For credit institutions classified as significant under Article 6(4) of Regulation (EU) No 1024/2013, Article 26(8) removes the internal-tester option: external testers only, meeting points (a) to (e) of Article 27(1). Every other identified entity may use internal testers if the authority approves the use, verifies dedicated resources and no conflicts of interest, and the threat intelligence provider is external. External testers are also required every third test — which is why a bank’s programme looks nothing like an insurer’s.

TLPT is a DORA instrument. NIS2 creates no TIBER-EU duty; it asks for evidence that the measures you claim actually work. If you are both a NIS2 entity and a financial entity, keep the two evidence sets separate — the NIS2 audit page covers the second one.

What the report proves, and to whom

  • To the supervisor. Article 26(6) requires that, once reports and remediation plans are agreed, you and where applicable the external testers give the designated authority a summary of the findings, the remediation plans and the documentation showing the test met the requirements. Article 26(7) obliges the authority to attest that it did, so other competent authorities recognise it, and obliges you to notify your own competent authority of the attestation, the findings summary and the remediation plans.
  • To the board. The attestation says the process was followed; the test summary report says which detection, escalation and response steps held — the first honest measure of whether monitoring spend bought anything. Under the comply-or-explain principle a deviation does not automatically invalidate a test, but it can affect whether the attestation is issued, so record the reasons.
  • To the auditor. Article 6(6) requires internal audit to review the ICT risk management framework, and a TLPT report is an artefact it can sample next to the register of information and the contracts. It is evidence about a point in time, not the current state.

What it does not prove: DORA compliance, security outside the agreed scope, or that findings were fixed. It is not an ISO/IEC 27001 certification and no guarantee of detection next year. The wider evidence picture is on the DORA in Estonia page, the review route on DORA audit.

Testers and independence requirements

Article 27(1) allows only testers that meet five conditions: highest suitability and reputability; demonstrated expertise in threat intelligence, penetration testing and red team testing; certification by an accreditation body in a Member State or adherence to formal codes of conduct or ethical frameworks; independent assurance or an audit report on how they manage the risks of the test, including protection of your confidential information; and full professional indemnity insurance, including against misconduct and negligence. Article 27(3) adds that the contract must require sound management of the results, so generating, storing, aggregating, reporting or destroying test data creates no new risk for you.

The TIBER-EU procurement guidance exists because “we have done red teaming” is not a qualification. Ask in writing for threat intelligence capability, red team history in production, independence from the systems under test, an insurance certificate and a record of working with a TIBER cyber team. Where the provider is also your managed security service provider or system integrator, document the conflict and how it is managed.

We do not present ourselves as a TIBER-EE tester. What we do is prepare the ground: penetration testing that clears ordinary weaknesses, an audit of logging and detection coverage so the blue team report measures something real, and an inventory of critical functions and ICT providers the scope document can be built on. The mandatory test needs a tester selected against the framework and agreed with Finantsinspektsioon.

Timeline and budget reality

TLPT is not a four-week booking. The framework gives indicative durations; most entities underestimate them, because nothing in the red team phase starts before the threat intelligence report is approved.

Two costs sit outside the table. Remediation: findings you cannot fix within a quarter become a second project with its own budget. Internal time: the control team, the blue team report and the replay exercise are your people. There is no public price list, because the drivers are critical functions in scope, threat intelligence depth, production risk, third parties and remediation volume. Ask every proposal for the scope boundary, the named team, the cadence, who owns the artefacts the authority reviews and what is excluded.

Common failure modes

  • Booked as a large penetration test. No intelligence phase, no validated scope, no replay: excellent testing, no attestation.
  • Scope written by the tester. Article 26(2) makes the choice of critical or important functions your task, and the authority validates the result; a tool-led target list does not satisfy it.
  • Outsourced systems left out. The article explicitly includes functions contracted to ICT third-party providers; their participation is arranged, not assumed.
  • A blue team that was told. Once the SOC knows, the detection measurement is gone and the blue team report is fiction.
  • Procurement at the last minute. Providers must be in place before the testing phase opens, and due diligence against Article 27 takes longer than a purchase order.
  • No stop conditions. Article 26(5) requires controls that limit impact on data, assets and critical functions; stop conditions and a rollback plan belong in the initiation pack.
  • Logging switched off. Without retained logs there is no replay, no blue team report and nothing to show the authority.
  • Remediation without owners or dates. The attestation arrives, the findings age, and the next test measures the same gaps.

Frequently asked questions

What is TLPT and what is TIBER-EU?

TLPT is threat-led penetration testing: a controlled, intelligence-led attack on the live production systems behind a financial entity’s critical or important functions, required by Article 26 of DORA from the entities the competent authority identifies. TIBER-EU is the European framework that describes how such a test is run, published by the ECB and updated in 2024 to align with the DORA regulatory technical standards. In Estonia the national implementation is TIBER-EE.

Is TIBER-EU the same thing as TLPT?

No. TLPT is the obligation in Article 26 of DORA; TIBER-EU is one way to discharge it, and the framework the DORA technical standards were written against. A TIBER-EU test therefore satisfies TLPT, but TLPT is a legal requirement that exists whether or not a test uses the TIBER-EU label, and it is the national implementation document that tells you how the authority will supervise it.

Who has to run a DORA TLPT?

Financial entities other than those referred to in the first subparagraph of Article 16(1) and other than microenterprises, which the competent authority has identified under Article 26(8) and to which it has issued a supervisory decision. The authority weighs impact on the financial sector, systemic character and the ICT risk profile. In Estonia Finantsinspektsioon is the TLPT authority for the entities in its remit; for significant institutions under direct ECB supervision the ECB is.

How is threat-led penetration testing different from red teaming?

Red teaming is the technique: testers acting as an adversary against objectives you agreed. TLPT wraps that technique in a regulated process — entity-specific threat intelligence first, a scope that covers critical or important functions and is validated by the authority, a blue team that is not told, a closure phase with a test summary report and a remediation plan, and an attestation from the TLPT authority. A red team exercise with none of that is legitimate testing, but it is not TLPT.

How often must a TLPT be repeated?

At least every three years under Article 26(1), and the competent authority may ask for a shorter interval or allow a longer one when the risk profile and operational circumstances justify it. If you use internal testers, Article 26(8) also requires external testers every third test, and significant credit institutions may use external testers only.

Does the attestation prove DORA compliance?

No. Article 26(7) requires the authority to attest that the test was performed in accordance with the requirements, so the result can be recognised by other competent authorities. That proves the process, not your security, and it says nothing about incident reporting, the register of information, contract clauses or the rest of the DORA duties.

Can Tehisabiline run a TLPT?

We do not present ourselves as a TIBER-EE tester. We run ordinary penetration tests and cyber audits that prepare the ground — scope, asset and provider inventory, logging and detection gaps, remediation evidence — but they do not replace a mandatory TLPT, for which the tester must be selected against the framework and agreed with Finantsinspektsioon.
