ISO/IEC 27001 · clause 9.2

ISO 27001 internal audit checklist and gap analysis

Clause 9.2 decides whether your internal audit is defensible, not the checklist. This page gives the requirement, a six-step method, a 63-row checklist across the four Annex A themes and a scoring sheet that reflects exposure and repeat findings instead of counting rows.

Short answer

An internal audit under clause 9.2 of ISO/IEC 27001:2022 is a planned check of your own information security management system (ISMS), carried out by people who are not responsible for what they are checking. It answers two questions: does the ISMS conform to the requirements of the standard and to your own rules, and is it effectively implemented and maintained. It is not a certification audit, not a penetration test, and not a review of the policy library. The output is a set of findings with evidence, reported to relevant management and recorded so that the management review under clause 9.3 can act on it.

Clause 9.2 requirements

Clause 9.2 is short. Almost everything that makes an internal audit weak sits in the gap between the clause and what an organisation actually does, so the table below pairs each requirement with the artefact a reviewer asks for.

RequirementWhat clause 9.2 asks forWhat I sample to test it
FrequencyAudits at planned intervals. The standard sets no number: the programme fixes frequency, methods, responsibilities, planning requirements and reporting.The programme document, then every report actually produced in the last 12 months against the dates it promised.
ScopeEach audit has a defined scope; over the programme the whole ISMS is covered, including clauses 4 to 10 and the controls in the Statement of Applicability.The scopes of the last four audits plotted against the Statement of Applicability, to find controls nobody has ever audited.
CriteriaThe audit states what conformity means for that audit: the standard, your own policies and standards, and any legal or contractual duty you have adopted.The criteria line in the audit plan, and whether each finding quotes a requirement taken from it.
Auditor independenceAuditors are selected so that the audit process is objective and impartial; nobody audits their own work.The auditor assignment for the last two cycles against the list of process owners, and the CV or training record behind each name.
Reporting to managementResults are reported to relevant management and recorded; audit results are among the required inputs to the management review under clause 9.3.The distribution record, the management review minutes, and whether the actions in them are the ones the audit raised.
Documented informationEvidence of the audit programme and of the audit results must exist: plan, criteria, checklists, findings, report, corrective actions.One complete audit file, from the plan to a corrective action closed with evidence.

The clause does not prescribe a frequency, a sample size, a report template, a severity scale or a taxonomy of findings. Those are yours to define, and a certification auditor will read them as your own criteria. Write them once and keep them stable: a scoring method that changes every cycle makes the trend unreadable.

How to run the audit in six steps

  1. Write the programme, not a single audit. One page: the cycle, the areas to cover, who audits what, when it lands, and how coverage maps to the Statement of Applicability. Without the coverage map the programme will quietly audit access control three times and the physical site never.
  2. Fix scope and criteria per audit. Name the systems, locations and processes, then name the criteria: clauses 4 to 10 of the standard, your own policies, the Statement of Applicability, and any legal duty you have adopted. Estonia adds regulatory obligations of its own, and a control that answers both saves a cycle.
  3. Pick auditors who cannot defend their own work. Swap areas between two people, use another unit, or engage an external auditor. In a five-person company there is no internal independence, so the honest options are a peer from outside the ISMS or someone hired for the day. The organisation keeps the responsibility either way.
  4. Sample from a population you listed first. Export the full list before you choose a sample, then take items from it rather than the ones offered to you. A defensible house default: five items per control, or the entire population when it holds fewer than ten; for access reviews, sample the last completed cycle rather than the one in progress.
  5. Record evidence as you go. For each row: the artefact identifier, the date, who showed it, what you saw, and the requirement it is measured against. Screenshots without a date and an owner are the reason findings get argued about three months later.
  6. Report, agree actions, close the loop. Findings go to the owners with dates, the report goes to relevant management, the results go into the management review, and the next cycle re-tests the same rows. A finding that reappears unchanged is a management problem, not a technical one.

One practical note on ordering: test the operational rows in the morning and the document rows in the afternoon. Documents can be produced for you in advance; a restore, an access export or a configured laptop cannot.

The checklist by Annex A theme

Annex A of the 2022 edition holds 93 controls in four themes: 37 organisational (5.x), 8 people (6.x), 14 physical (7.x) and 34 technological (8.x). Annex A is a normative annex, but it is not a requirement list: its controls apply when your risk assessment brings them into scope, and clause 6.1.3 requires the Statement of Applicability to record and justify the inclusions and exclusions. So the audit tests the ISMS you claim to run, using the Statement of Applicability as the index.

The tables below show the rows a first internal audit should not skip — 30 of the 63 in the CSV checklist, which carries the same audit question and evidence columns for every row, plus a result column (conforms, partial, gap, not tested) and a gap note.

The remaining rows — supplier agreements, threat intelligence, capacity, malware protection, configuration management, data deletion, code review and the rest — are in the CSV, grouped by theme, ready to sort and fill in. Where a row does not apply because the control is excluded from your Statement of Applicability, mark it as such rather than leaving it blank: blank rows are what makes a programme look untested.

Gap analysis and scoring

Counting rows measures coverage, not risk. Two audits with twenty gaps each can differ by an order of magnitude, and a report that lists forty findings in alphabetical order tells the board nothing. Score each gap on two things: what the control protects, and whether you are looking at the same problem again.

FactorLevelWhat puts a gap here
Exposure1Internal only, reversible, no customer or regulated data; a colleague notices within a day.
Exposure2Business-critical internal: finance, delivery, source code, or anything whose loss stops work for days.
Exposure3Externally reachable, or holding regulated or personal data, or required for continuity and incident reporting.
Repeat statusx1First time raised.
Repeat statusx1.5Raised in the previous cycle and not closed.
Repeat statusx2Third occurrence or later: treat as a major finding whatever the exposure.

Score = exposure × repeat. A score of 1 to 2 is minor and belongs in the report with a date. 3 to 4 is major: corrective action with an owner, a deadline and a re-test inside the quarter. 6 and above escalates to the management body under clause 9.3, because a control that has failed three cycles is not an implementation problem.

Worked example. Two findings from the same audit. Finding one: the restore test for the customer database was last performed eleven months ago and the log records a partial restore, so the stated RTO has never been demonstrated. Exposure 3, first occurrence, score 3 — major. Finding two: nine unlocked screens out of forty desks in one office, exposure 1, but the same finding appeared in the previous two cycles, so score 1 × 2 = 2, which reads minor by arithmetic while the repeat rule forces it up.

That is the entire point of the example: nine clear-desk observations are one systemic finding about culture and enforcement, not nine findings, and one untested restore outweighs all of them. Report the count of affected items as a sample result inside the finding, not as separate severity. Whoever reads the report should be able to see, in one line per finding, what breaks and how badly.

Two honest caveats. There is no ISO severity scale — this arithmetic is a house rule, and your procedure is the authority for it. And a scored gap is not yet a nonconformity until you can name the requirement it breaches, which is the next section.

Findings that are not nonconformities

A nonconformity is a requirement not met: a clause of the standard, your own policy, or a commitment recorded in the Statement of Applicability. If you cannot name that requirement, what you have is something else, and naming it correctly is what keeps the report credible.

  • Nonconformity. Requirement, evidence, effect. For example: Annex A 8.13 asks for backup copies of information, software and systems to be maintained and regularly tested; the sampled restore failed and no successful test exists in the period; the effect is an unproven recovery time for the customer database.
  • Observation. No requirement breached, but the condition is fragile — a control that works because one person remembers to run it after the deploy. Worth raising, not worth a corrective action with a deadline.
  • Opportunity for improvement. The requirement is met and the control could be cheaper, faster or more measurable. Log it so the next audit does not rediscover it as a gap.
  • Good practice worth keeping. Name it in the report. Audits that only record failure lose the reason a control was designed the way it was.

Major and minor are not defined by the standard for internal audits. Your procedure should define them, and if you borrow the certification body's definitions, borrow them deliberately: a certification body judges conformity for a certificate, while your internal audit exists to change something before a customer or a regulator finds it. Estonian entities that also carry NIS2 duties have a second consumer for these findings, which is why the mapping in NIS2 and ISO 27001 compared is worth keeping next to the report.

Certification audit vs internal audit

The two audits look similar on paper — someone asks questions, samples evidence and writes findings — and they are not interchangeable.

AspectInternal audit (clause 9.2)Certification audit
Who performs itYour own trained auditor, a peer from another unit, or an external auditor you engage.An accredited certification body, independent of your organisation.
PurposeShow whether the ISMS conforms and is effective, and produce corrective actions you own.Decide whether to issue a certificate, and keep deciding at each surveillance visit.
CriteriaThe standard, your own policies, your Statement of Applicability.The standard and your own ISMS documentation, as declared.
OutcomeFindings, severities and agreed actions; no external consequence.Nonconformities that must be closed before certification, or before the next surveillance.
TimingAt the intervals your programme sets, at least once before the certification audit.Two-stage initial audit, then surveillance and recertification on the body's accredited cycle.

Certification bodies in Estonia are accredited by the Estonian Accreditation Centre (EAK), and their audit and certification requirements come from the conformity-assessment standards their scheme applies, not from clause 9.2. The certification auditor will look at your internal audit records early, because a programme with no coverage gaps and closed findings is the strongest single piece of evidence that the ISMS is alive. An unused checklist is the opposite.

Two boundaries worth stating plainly. A certificate does not discharge a legal duty: NIS2 and, for financial entities, DORA impose their own reporting and supervision, supervised in Estonia by RIA and the Financial Supervision Authority respectively. And this page is a method, not a certification decision; when you want the technical half of the evidence — a scoped cybersecurity audit or a penetration test that stands up to a sampling auditor — that is a separate piece of work.

Frequently asked questions

What is an ISO 27001 internal audit checklist?

A working list of the questions an internal audit asks about each control in your Statement of Applicability, with the evidence that answers them. It supports clause 9.2: it does not replace the audit programme, the criteria or the auditor.

How often does ISO 27001 require an internal audit?

Clause 9.2 requires internal audits at planned intervals and leaves the interval to you. The requirement is that a programme exists, states its frequency, methods, responsibilities, planning requirements and reporting, and is actually followed. Many organisations cover the whole information security management system once a year in smaller quarterly slices.

Is a gap analysis the same as an internal audit?

No. A gap analysis compares where you are with what a standard requires, usually before implementation, to plan the work. An internal audit tests a management system that is supposed to be running and reports whether it conforms and is effective. You can do both in one project, but they answer different questions.

Do we have to audit all 93 Annex A controls?

No. You audit the controls in your Statement of Applicability, plus the requirements of clauses 4 to 10. Controls you excluded with a stated justification are not audited for implementation, although the justification itself can be questioned, because clause 6.1.3 requires the Statement of Applicability to be complete and defensible.

Can an external person perform our internal audit?

Yes. Clause 9.2 requires the audit process to be objective and impartial, not that the auditor is an employee. The organisation still owns the programme, the findings and the corrective actions, and whoever built or runs the information security management system should not audit their own work.

Does an internal auditor need an ISO 27001 certificate?

No. The standard sets no qualification requirement for internal auditors and requires no certificate of any kind. It requires that the audit is planned, that the criteria are stated, and that the auditor is competent to test the subject. A certificate is one way to show competence, not the only one, and clause 9.2 does not ask for it.

What belongs in the internal audit report?

Scope, criteria, dates, auditors, the populations and sample sizes used, each finding with the requirement it breaches and the evidence seen, the severity you assigned, and agreed actions with owners and dates. The results then have to reach relevant management and appear in the management review record under clause 9.3.
